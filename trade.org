** Chinese stock market: key characteristics

*** Scope and structure
- *Mainland A-shares*: Shanghai (SSE), Shenzhen (SZSE), Beijing (BSE). RMB-denominated, mostly domestic investors.
- *B-shares*: foreign-currency shares, largely obsolete/illiquid.
- *Hong Kong H-shares / red chips / P-chips*: offshore listings of Chinese companies.
- *US ADRs / VIE structures*: many tech/internet names, subject to geopolitical and audit risk.
- *Regulators*: CSRC, PBOC, SAFE, NDRC, State Council. Policy signals often matter more than fundamentals in the short run.

*** Trading rules and frictions
- *T+1*: you cannot sell A-shares bought the same day.
- *Price limits*: main board ±10%, ChiNext/STAR ±20%, BSE ±30%. ST stocks ±5%.
- *Lot size*: usually 100 shares.
- *Short selling*: possible via margin/securities lending, but constrained and often expensive.
- *IPO regime*: moved from approval-based to registration-based (STAR, ChiNext, BSE, then main board). Still heavy state influence.
- *Delisting*: historically rare, now increasing, but still not US-like.
- *Trading suspensions*: historically used to avoid crashes; less common now but still a risk.

*** Investor base
- *Retail investors*: dominate trading volume (roughly 60–80% depending on period), high turnover, momentum/theme-driven.
- *Institutions*: public mutual funds, private funds, quant funds, insurance, pensions, National Social Security Fund.
- *State players*: Central Huijin, China Securities Finance Corp (“national team”) can intervene to stabilize markets.
- *Foreign investors*: via Stock Connect (Northbound/Southbound), QFII/RQFII. Northbound flow is a watched signal.

*** Market composition and sectors
- *Indices*: SSE Composite, CSI 300, CSI 500, CSI 1000, ChiNext, STAR 50, Hang Seng, MSCI China.
- *Heavy weights*: financials (banks, insurance, brokers), industrials, materials, energy, consumer staples (especially liquor), healthcare.
- *Growth themes*: EVs, batteries, solar, semiconductors, AI, defense, high-dividend SOEs.
- *Distressed areas*: real estate, local government financing vehicles, some consumer discretionary.

*** Earnings prospects and macro
- *Macro*: slowing growth, property crisis, local debt overhang, deflationary pressure, weak consumer confidence, export resilience, geopolitical tension.
- *Policy themes*: “common prosperity,” SOE reform, “Sino-specific valuation” (中特估), high dividend, tech self-sufficiency, stimulus.
- *Sector outlooks*:
  - *Banks/SOEs*: cheap, high dividend, but slow growth and hidden credit risk.
  - *Consumer*: recovery uncertain; premium liquor resilient, mass market under pressure.
  - *Tech/Semis*: policy support vs. US restrictions; high volatility.
  - *New energy*: oversupply and price wars in solar/EV, but long-term growth.
  - *Real estate*: restructuring, high risk, policy-dependent.

*** Is it “gambling” or “rigged”?
- *Not purely random*: returns show factor structure (size, value, reversal, momentum, quality, low volatility, turnover), policy cycles, liquidity effects, and sentiment cycles.
- *But structurally disadvantaged for retail*:
  - Information asymmetry (insiders, state, large funds).
  - T+1, price limits, shorting constraints create asymmetric payoffs.
  - IPO underpricing, lockups, insider selling, related-party transactions.
  - Weak minority shareholder protection, accounting fraud (e.g., Kangmei), earnings management.
  - Policy intervention and “national team” buying can distort prices.
  - Market manipulation, “pump and dump,” and theme speculation are common.
- *Verdict*: it is not a literal casino, but it is a heavily regulated, policy-driven, friction-heavy market where retail often loses. A Bayesian statistician can have an edge, but only with:
  - Point-in-time data, survivorship-bias-free universes.
  - Realistic trading rules (T+1, limits, costs, capacity).
  - Regime-switching models, robust priors, Bayesian model averaging.
  - Awareness of non-stationarity and policy shocks.
  - Niche strategies: small-cap quant, event-driven, AH/ETF arbitrage, convertible bonds, statistical arb.

These are *price adjustment methods* for {{dividends, splits, and rights issues}@0}. They remove artificial gaps so charts and returns are continuous.

qfq anchors to the latest, hfq anchors to the {{earliest}@1}.

- *For trading*: use raw prices for orders, limits, and execution.
- *For charting*: use qfq for clean technical patterns and current price context.
- *For backtesting/returns*: use hfq or raw + cumulative adjustment factors.
- *For quant*: store raw prices and 复权因子 (adjustment factors). Compute total returns yourself. This avoids vendor inconsistencies and look-ahead bias.
- *Pitfalls*: vendors differ on dividend tax, rights issues, and rounding. qfq can even show negative historical prices if cumulative dividends exceed the price.

Never use qfq for long-term return calculations unless you explicitly account for the changing anchor.
*** 不复权 (raw)
Actual traded prices. Use for:
- Order execution, limit prices, actual market microstructure.
- Volume, spread, tax lots.

*** 前复权 (qfq) — “forward/previous adjustment”
- *Anchor*: the *latest* price is unchanged (equals raw current price).
- *Historical prices* are adjusted downward/upward to remove ex-dividend/ex-rights gaps.
- *Useful for*: technical analysis, chart patterns, current price reference, setting alerts, visual continuity.
- *Caveat*: qfq history *changes* whenever a new dividend/split occurs, because the anchor is the present. So cached qfq data becomes stale.

*** 后复权 (hfq) — “backward/post adjustment”
- *Anchor*: the *earliest* price is unchanged.
- *Later prices* are adjusted upward to reflect cumulative dividends/splits.
- *Useful for*: backtesting, performance measurement, total return calculation, long-term charts, comparing cumulative returns across stocks.
- *Caveat*: hfq prices are not real traded prices and are often much higher than raw.

*** Simple example (one cash dividend, D=5)
Raw: Day0 = 100, Day1 ex = 95, Day2 = 100.

| Day | Raw | qfq | hfq |
|-----|-----|-----|-----|
| 0   | 100 | 95  | 100 |
| 1   | 95  | 95  | 100 |
| 2   | 100 | 100 | 105.26 |

- qfq removes the drop on Day1; current price stays raw.
- hfq keeps Day0=100 and scales later prices by the cumulative adjustment factor (assuming dividend reinvestment). The total return from Day0 to Day2 is 5.26% in both qfq and hfq.

    beliefs = signal(market)

** define the request codes and functions

We access both the endpoints with the same protocol.

#+name: source-get
#+begin_src python
def get(url: str, *, encoding: str = "utf-8") -> str:
    """One GET, retried, so a dropped connection is not a data gap."""
    for attempt in range(RETRIES):
        try:
            with urllib.request.urlopen(
                    urllib.request.Request(url, headers=HEADERS),
                    timeout=20) as response:
                return response.read().decode(encoding, errors="replace")
        except (urllib.error.URLError, OSError):
            if attempt == RETRIES - 1:
                raise
            time.sleep(DELAY * 2 ** attempt)
    raise AssertionError("unreachable")
#+end_src

#+RESULTS: source-get

We access the endpoints very politely: =DELAY= paces symbol requests, and the retry sleeps =DELAY * 2 ** attempt= between attempts, so a dropped connection costs a retry instead of a hole in the snapshot and a full refresh stays a background chore rather than something that looks like an attack.

=RemoteDisconnected= is a subclass of =OSError=, so the retry catches the same failures that make the eastmoney route hopeless. The difference is that this endpoint answers on the second try.

def write_bars(symbol: str, adjust: str, path: Path) -> None:
    """Fetch, normalize, and store one complete daily series."""
    span = f"1900-01-01,{date.today().isoformat()}"
    code = market_code(symbol)
    url = f"{KLINE}?param={code},day,{span},{DEPTH},{adjust}"

    path.parent.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
    with open(path, "w", newline="") as handle:
        writer = csv.writer(handle)
        writer.writerow(("date", "open", "high", "low", "close", "volume"))
        writer.writerows(
            (row[0], row[1], row[3], row[4], row[2], int(float(row[5])))
            for row in rows
        )
    print(f"{adjust or 'raw':>3} {symbol} {len(rows):>4} bars "
          f"{rows[0][0]} .. {rows[-1][0]}")

*** write instrument names

=names.csv= is one shared file, so a subset refresh must preserve names for symbols not included in the request. The quote endpoint batches symbols and returns fixed-width, semicolon-separated records; =write_names= fetches, parses, merges, and writes in one pass.

#+name: source-write-names
#+begin_src python
def write_names(symbols: tuple[str, ...], path: Path) -> None:
    """Fetch, merge, and store names for the selected symbols."""
    codes = [market_code(symbol) for symbol in symbols]
    fetched: dict[str, str] = {}
    for start in range(0, len(codes), BATCH):
        chunk = codes[start:start + BATCH]
        text = get(QUOTE + ",".join(chunk), encoding="gbk")
        for line in text.split(";"):
            fields = line.split("~")
            if len(fields) > 3 and fields[2].isdigit():
                fetched[fields[2]] = "".join(fields[1].split())

    stored: dict[str, str] = {}
    if path.exists():
        with open(path, newline="") as handle:
            stored = {row["symbol"]: row["name"]
                      for row in csv.DictReader(handle)}
    names = stored | fetched
    path.parent.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
    with open(path, "w", newline="") as handle:
        writer = csv.writer(handle)
        writer.writerow(("symbol", "name"))
        writer.writerows(sorted(names.items()))
    print(f"names {len(names)} symbols stored")
#+end_src

#+RESULTS: source-write-names

Names are padded to fit the quote protocol's fixed-width fields, so whitespace is removed inside each name as it is parsed. Fetched names win; names outside a subset refresh remain in the merged table.

def refresh(symbols: tuple[str, ...] = (),
            series: tuple[str, ...] = SERIES) -> None:

    symbols = symbols or WATCHLIST
    for name in series:
        for symbol in symbols:

#+name: market-instrument
#+begin_src python
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Instrument:
    """One tradable name and its relevant information."""

    symbol: str
    name: str
    bars: Bars
    adjusted: Bars = ()

    @property
    def closes(self) -> tuple[Money, ...]:
        """Unadjusted closes, oldest first: the mark's input."""
        return tuple(bar.close for bar in self.bars)

    @property
    def adjusted_closes(self) -> tuple[Money, ...]:
        """Dividend-continuous closes: for returns, never for marks."""
        return tuple(bar.close for bar in self.adjusted)

type Market = dict[str, Instrument]     # symbol -> instrument
#+end_src

replay ledger to get as_of account, current prices read from =gather= results.

The booking consists of three parts: the static account (holding at any specific moment), the dynamic ledger (the total trading history), and how to replay the ledger to get the account.

This is the state half of the vocabulary: what you hold, and the history that produced it. Risk *policy* is deliberately not here -- lot size and the weight caps belong to =Rules= in =decide.py=, because they are choices about how to trade rather than facts about the account.

An account is stored as its history -- a deposit, then fills -- and every snapshot is *derived* by replaying that history to a date. Nothing is entered by hand or carried forward: =replay(ledger, as_of)= answers "what did the account look like then" from the same tuple at any moment, which is what a backtest needs and what keeps the state from drifting away from what was actually executed.

The account opens with one 50K cash flow and no positions. There is deliberately no reconstructed broker history: the first fill appended is the first thing the account ever did, so every later number traces back to an execution rather than to a transcription.

A position is a *bag of lots* rather than a quantity and an average price. That one choice is what makes FIFO cost basis and per-lot T+1 availability fall out of the data instead of being tracked alongside it.

** the account

(Money + Quantity) -> Lot -> Position -> Account.

An Account is a point-in-time holding: cash, plus the open positions, plus the derived numbers the decision layer reads -- equity, marked value, weights.

A Position records all the trading actions (in Lots) executed up to that moment, plus the reference price it is marked at.

Lot, Position, and Account are all frozen dataclasses. Immutability makes them better suited for functional programming, and makes the replays a safer operation.

*** money and shares

=Money= and =Quantity= are imported from =market=, not redefined here: a price is market data before it is account state, and one money type in the project means =Bar.close= and =Account.cash= are the same =float= with no conversion between them. That is the only dependency edge between the two modules, and it points from =book= into =market=.

Money is a plain =float=. At this volume -- tens of fills, prices with two decimals, capital under 100K -- float is comfortably exact for the arithmetic that matters: quantities are small integers, so =quantity * price= is correctly rounded, and a sum of a few hundred such terms accumulates no visible error.

What is *not* guaranteed is exactness after a division, so the rule for assertions is a split, and it is used consistently below:

- *Exact =* for quantities, cash, and marked value. These are integer sums, so === is meaningful and a mismatch is a real bug.
- =math.isclose= for anything divided -- cost per share, weights, returns. These are correctly rounded but not exactly representable, so === would be a coin flip dressed as a test. The probability layer will need the same discipline for every ratio it produces.

If the ledger ever reconciles against a broker statement to the fen, that is the moment to revisit this and move to integer cents. Until then, float keeps the source readable.

*** the position

#+name: book-position
#+begin_src python
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Position:
    """A holding in one instrument: its lots, its name, and its mark."""

    symbol: str
    name: str
    lots: tuple[Lot, ...]
    price: Money

    @property
    def quantity(self) -> Quantity:
        return sum(lot.quantity for lot in self.lots)

    @property
    def value(self) -> Money:
        """Marked value: what the position is worth right now."""
        return self.quantity * self.price

    @property
    def cost(self) -> Money:
        """Average cost per share, weighted over the lots."""
        total = sum(lot.quantity * lot.price for lot in self.lots)
        return total / self.quantity if self.quantity else 0.0

    @property
    def pnl(self) -> Money:
        """Unrealised profit and loss against the cost basis."""
        return self.value - self.cost * self.quantity

    def available(self, as_of: date) -> Quantity:
        """Shares sellable under T+1: whose lot predates ``as_of``."""
        return sum(lot.quantity for lot in self.lots
                   if lot.purchase_date < as_of)
#+end_src

#+RESULTS: book-position

=price= is the mark the position was valued at, supplied by the caller because the book does not know about markets. Storing it rather than looking it up makes =value= a total property, so one position cannot be priced two different ways inside one session.

=available= is *strictly* before =as_of=, so a lot bought today is not sellable today; and the date is a parameter rather than a clock reading, so the same position answers for any past or future moment. =cost= is a weighted average over the lots and is derived rather than stored -- no stored average can disagree with the lots it came from.

#+name: book-account
#+begin_src python
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Account:
    """Cash plus open positions: the account at one moment."""

    cash: Money
    positions: tuple[Position, ...]

    @property
    def value(self) -> Money:
        """Marked value of the positions alone, excluding cash."""
        return sum(position.value for position in self.positions)

    @property
    def equity(self) -> Money:
        return self.cash + self.value

    @property
    def cash_weight(self) -> Money:
        """Cash as a share of equity, zero for an emptied account."""
        return self.cash / self.equity if self.equity else 0.0

    def position(self, symbol: str) -> Position:
        """The position in ``symbol``; a missing one is a lookup error."""
        for position in self.positions:
            if position.symbol == symbol:
                return position
        raise KeyError(f"no position for symbol: {symbol}")

    def weight(self, symbol: str) -> Money:
        """The position's share of equity, zero for an emptied account."""
        return self.position(symbol).value / self.equity if self.equity else 0.0
#+end_src

#+RESULTS: book-account

=Account= is state only. Every risk limit lives in =decide.Rules=, so this type cannot quietly carry a policy into a function that has no business seeing one.

There is no duplicate-symbol check, unlike the old =Portfolio=. Positions come from =replay='s dict of symbol to lots, and a dict cannot contain a duplicate key, so such a check would guard a state that cannot occur.

=weight= and =cash_weight= are 0 for an emptied account rather than a division error. Equity *is* cash plus marked value, so zero equity means every weight is zero; dividing anyway would raise where the state itself is perfectly describable.

** replay the ledger into account

cash_delta + (sell_lots -> lot_book) -> replay

Replay is two independent folds, which is the nicest part of the design.

1. =cash_delta(entry)= gives each entry's signed cash effect, and cash is just their sum. A sale's proceeds do not depend on which lot it consumed, so cash never needs to know about matching.
2. =lot_book(fills)= is the FIFO fold of the fills alone. =sell_lots(lots, quantity, as_of)= consumes the open lots oldest first, skipping any lot not yet available under T+1, and raising if the sale cannot be covered rather than inventing shares. Symbols sold out entirely are dropped, so the book is exactly the set of open positions.

=replay= is deliberately not given the market, only the two lookups it needs. A symbol missing from =names= or =prices= raises =KeyError=, and that is the failure we want: a holding the market does not know about is a bug downstream, not a position worth zero.

Entries dated after =as_of= are ignored, so one ledger answers for any moment -- that is the backtest door.

*** the signed cash effect

#+name: book-cash-delta
#+begin_src python
def cash_delta(entry: Entry) -> Money:
    """The signed cash effect of one entry, independent of the lot book."""
    match entry:
        case CashFlow(amount=amount):
            return amount
        case Fill(side=side, quantity=quantity, price=price):
            return side.sign * -quantity * price
#+end_src

#+RESULTS: book-cash-delta

Cash never needs to know which lot a sale consumed, so this is the sum of the flows rather than a side effect of matching. The =match= makes that literal: the two cases are exhaustive over =Entry=, so there is no branch for "some other kind of entry".

#+name: book-sell-lots
#+begin_src python
def sell_lots(lots: tuple[Lot, ...], quantity: Quantity, as_of: date) -> tuple[Lot, ...]:
    """Consume available lots in FIFO order, oldest first."""
    remaining = quantity
    result = []
    for lot in lots:
        consumed = min(lot.quantity, remaining) \
            if remaining and lot.purchase_date < as_of else 0
        remaining -= consumed
        if lot.quantity > consumed:
            result.append(Lot(lot.purchase_date, lot.quantity - consumed, lot.price))
    if remaining:
        raise ValueError(f"not enough shares available to sell: {remaining}")
    return tuple(result)
#+end_src

#+RESULTS: book-sell-lots

A lot bought on =as_of= is not yet available under T+1, so it is skipped. If the older lots cannot cover the sale, the ledger is wrong and we say so loudly rather than inventing shares.

*** folding fills into a lot book

#+name: book-lot-book
#+begin_src python
def lot_book(fills: tuple[Fill, ...]) -> dict[str, tuple[Lot, ...]]:
    """Fold fills into open lots per symbol: buys open, sells consume FIFO."""
    book: dict[str, list[Lot]] = {}
    for fill in fills:
        if fill.side is Side.BUY:
            book.setdefault(fill.symbol, []).append(
                Lot(fill.date, fill.quantity, fill.price))
        else:
            book[fill.symbol] = list(
                sell_lots(tuple(book.get(fill.symbol, ())), fill.quantity, fill.date)
            )
    return {symbol: tuple(lots) for symbol, lots in book.items() if lots}
#+end_src

lot_book processes each fill in order and maintains a per-symbol “open lot” book:

- A *BUY* appends a new open =Lot(date, quantity, price)= for that symbol.
- A *SELL* does not append a lot. Instead it calls =sell_lots(...)= to consume existing lots FIFO for that symbol, then *replaces* the symbol’s list with the remaining lots.
- At the end it converts each symbol’s remaining list of lots to a tuple, and removes symbols with no remaining lots.

The =append= call is applied to the list stored in =book= for =fill.symbol= — not to =book= itself.

#+begin_src python

*** the replay

Cash is one running balance: the sum of the =CashFlow= entries and the fills.

#+name: book-replay
#+begin_src python
def replay(ledger: Ledger, as_of: date, *,
           names: dict[str, str],
           prices: dict[str, Money]) -> Account:
    """Fold the ledger into the account state at ``as_of``."""
    entries = [entry for entry in ledger if entry.date <= as_of]
    cash = sum(cash_delta(entry) for entry in entries)
    lots = lot_book(tuple(entry for entry in entries if isinstance(entry, Fill)))
    positions = tuple(
        Position(symbol, names[symbol], open_lots, prices[symbol])
        for symbol, open_lots in lots.items()
    )
    return Account(cash, positions)
#+end_src

#+RESULTS: book-replay

Two things about the shape are deliberate. The prices and names are keyword-only, so a caller cannot swap them by position. And the filter is =entry.date<=as_of=, not =<=, because an entry dated exactly =as_of= has happened by the end of that day.

** infer: investment beliefs

=infer.py=. =infer(market)= turns a =Market= into =Beliefs=, a =dict[str, Belief]= keyed by symbol.

#+begin_src python
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Belief:
    symbol: str
    expected_return: Price       # over the window in `closes`
#+end_src

Two fields is the whole discipline of this layer: =rebalance= can only see the =Belief=, never the raw prices. The current model is naive momentum -- the return over the observed window -- i.e. "the recent past repeats". It is deliberately dumb, but it is structurally real: one number per name, computed from the market and nothing else. Swap the body, keep the signature.

Two things about this model changed when =gather= became real, and neither was a code change:

- The window is now =gather='s =WINDOW= (60 trading days, about a quarter) rather than "however many closes the fixture happened to have". =closes[0] / closes[-1]= always meant "the return over the window"; it just was not obvious that the window was a *choice* until the window became larger than four.
- It reads =closes=, i.e. raw unadjusted closes, and ignores both =adjusted_closes= and the rest of the OHLCV bars. That is now a modelling decision with a cost: raw closes carry ex-dividend jumps, and 高=low=, =open=, and =volume= are all unread. The data is in =Instrument=; the signals are not yet.

The inference vocabulary:

- [ ] Signal: evidence, or a transformed feature
- [x] Belief: current probabilistic state, after combining evidence and prior info (one =expected_return= so far)
- [ ] Prediction: a forecast, tied to an instrument, horizon, target, and probability distribution, and possibly calibration information.
- [ ] Scenario: a coherent possible future path or portfolio outcome.

A managerie of strategies can be freely selected and combined later, as long as they all reduce to a =Belief=. The obvious first portfolio of them: a window chosen per horizon rather than fixed, returns from =adjusted_closes= instead of =closes=, and a volatility estimate from =high= and =low= -- which is what the OHLCV bars were fetched for.

** rebalance: beliefs into orders

=rebalance.py=. =rebalance(portfolio, beliefs, market, as_of)= turns beliefs and constraints into a batch of =Order=.

#+begin_src python
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Order:
    symbol: str
    name: str
    side: Side
    quantity: int                # whole lots, or a full odd-lot exit
    price: Price                 # reference mark, for the ticket only
    reason: str                  # human-readable "why", shown on the ticket
#+end_src

An order is an intention, not an execution. =Order.value= is =quantity * price=, and slippage will be exactly =fill.price - order.price=, which is why an order carries a reference price and nothing else about the future.

- =sell_order(portfolio, position, beliefs, as_of)= exits a name whose =expected_return= has turned non-positive (using =Position.available=, since T+1 applies on the way out too), or trims it back to the cap with =Portfolio.shares_to_sell_to_cap=. =legal_sell= then makes the size legal: whole lots, except that an odd-lot holding may be sold in full when the order closes it (the A-share 零股 rule). Without it a partial sell could emit 650 of 750 shares, which no broker accepts.
- =buy_orders(portfolio, beliefs, market, sells)= spends the cash the sells free, one equal slot per new name, ranked by =expected_return=. Three details worth keeping: a name whose smallest legal lot already breaks the cap is dropped *before* the budget is split (otherwise it silently eats cash it can never spend); the budget is clamped to one cap per new name, because trading at the mark leaves equity unchanged and an unclamped slot put 90% of a cash-rich account into a single name; and lot counts use =to_integral_value(ROUND_FLOOR)= because =int(x / y)= truncates toward zero and =round()= is banker's rounding.
- Sells come first: in the A-share market the proceeds are immediately reusable for buying, they just cannot be withdrawn until T+1.
- =rebalance= drops zero-quantity sell orders, so a "do nothing" name leaves no line on the ticket.
- =check_batch(portfolio, orders, market, as_of)= asserts what the batch itself promises -- legal lot sizes, sells before buys, no buy above the cap, no negative post-trade quantity -- and *returns* whatever T+1 makes unfixable rather than asserting it away. A same-day 30% position cannot be trimmed today; that is risk to report, not a crash.

* backtesting

The one design decision that makes backtesting possible is already everywhere: functions take =as_of= instead of reading a clock. =replay= filters the ledger at =as_of=, =Position.available= and =Portfolio.shares_to_sell_to_cap= use it for T+1, and =trade(as_of)= threads it through to =gather(as_of)=, so a session cannot see a bar after its own date.

The dated data source is now real: =gather(as_of)= answers from the stored snapshot, so replaying a past date is a matter of feeding hypothetical fills back into a ledger. The snapshot already holds 640 bars per name, so there is history to backtest over without fetching anything.
