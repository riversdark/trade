** Chinese stock market: key characteristics

*** Scope and structure
- *Mainland A-shares*: Shanghai (SSE), Shenzhen (SZSE), Beijing (BSE). RMB-denominated, mostly domestic investors.
- *B-shares*: foreign-currency shares, largely obsolete/illiquid.
- *Hong Kong H-shares / red chips / P-chips*: offshore listings of Chinese companies.
- *US ADRs / VIE structures*: many tech/internet names, subject to geopolitical and audit risk.
- *Regulators*: CSRC, PBOC, SAFE, NDRC, State Council. Policy signals often matter more than fundamentals in the short run.

*** Trading rules and frictions
- *T+1*: you cannot sell A-shares bought the same day.
- *Price limits*: main board ±10%, ChiNext/STAR ±20%, BSE ±30%. ST stocks ±5%.
- *Lot size*: usually 100 shares.
- *Short selling*: possible via margin/securities lending, but constrained and often expensive.
- *IPO regime*: moved from approval-based to registration-based (STAR, ChiNext, BSE, then main board). Still heavy state influence.
- *Delisting*: historically rare, now increasing, but still not US-like.
- *Trading suspensions*: historically used to avoid crashes; less common now but still a risk.

*** Investor base
- *Retail investors*: dominate trading volume (roughly 60–80% depending on period), high turnover, momentum/theme-driven.
- *Institutions*: public mutual funds, private funds, quant funds, insurance, pensions, National Social Security Fund.
- *State players*: Central Huijin, China Securities Finance Corp (“national team”) can intervene to stabilize markets.
- *Foreign investors*: via Stock Connect (Northbound/Southbound), QFII/RQFII. Northbound flow is a watched signal.

*** Market composition and sectors
- *Indices*: SSE Composite, CSI 300, CSI 500, CSI 1000, ChiNext, STAR 50, Hang Seng, MSCI China.
- *Heavy weights*: financials (banks, insurance, brokers), industrials, materials, energy, consumer staples (especially liquor), healthcare.
- *Growth themes*: EVs, batteries, solar, semiconductors, AI, defense, high-dividend SOEs.
- *Distressed areas*: real estate, local government financing vehicles, some consumer discretionary.

*** Earnings prospects and macro
- *Macro*: slowing growth, property crisis, local debt overhang, deflationary pressure, weak consumer confidence, export resilience, geopolitical tension.
- *Policy themes*: “common prosperity,” SOE reform, “Sino-specific valuation” (中特估), high dividend, tech self-sufficiency, stimulus.
- *Sector outlooks*:
  - *Banks/SOEs*: cheap, high dividend, but slow growth and hidden credit risk.
  - *Consumer*: recovery uncertain; premium liquor resilient, mass market under pressure.
  - *Tech/Semis*: policy support vs. US restrictions; high volatility.
  - *New energy*: oversupply and price wars in solar/EV, but long-term growth.
  - *Real estate*: restructuring, high risk, policy-dependent.

*** Is it “gambling” or “rigged”?
- *Not purely random*: returns show factor structure (size, value, reversal, momentum, quality, low volatility, turnover), policy cycles, liquidity effects, and sentiment cycles.
- *But structurally disadvantaged for retail*:
  - Information asymmetry (insiders, state, large funds).
  - T+1, price limits, shorting constraints create asymmetric payoffs.
  - IPO underpricing, lockups, insider selling, related-party transactions.
  - Weak minority shareholder protection, accounting fraud (e.g., Kangmei), earnings management.
  - Policy intervention and “national team” buying can distort prices.
  - Market manipulation, “pump and dump,” and theme speculation are common.
- *Verdict*: it is not a literal casino, but it is a heavily regulated, policy-driven, friction-heavy market where retail often loses. A Bayesian statistician can have an edge, but only with:
  - Point-in-time data, survivorship-bias-free universes.
  - Realistic trading rules (T+1, limits, costs, capacity).
  - Regime-switching models, robust priors, Bayesian model averaging.
  - Awareness of non-stationarity and policy shocks.
  - Niche strategies: small-cap quant, event-driven, AH/ETF arbitrage, convertible bonds, statistical arb.

These are *price adjustment methods* for {{dividends, splits, and rights issues}@0}. They remove artificial gaps so charts and returns are continuous.

qfq anchors to the latest, hfq anchors to the {{earliest}@1}.

- *For trading*: use raw prices for orders, limits, and execution.
- *For charting*: use qfq for clean technical patterns and current price context.
- *For backtesting/returns*: use hfq or raw + cumulative adjustment factors.
- *For quant*: store raw prices and 复权因子 (adjustment factors). Compute total returns yourself. This avoids vendor inconsistencies and look-ahead bias.
- *Pitfalls*: vendors differ on dividend tax, rights issues, and rounding. qfq can even show negative historical prices if cumulative dividends exceed the price.

Never use qfq for long-term return calculations unless you explicitly account for the changing anchor.
*** 不复权 (raw)
Actual traded prices. Use for:
- Order execution, limit prices, actual market microstructure.
- Volume, spread, tax lots.

*** 前复权 (qfq) — “forward/previous adjustment”
- *Anchor*: the *latest* price is unchanged (equals raw current price).
- *Historical prices* are adjusted downward/upward to remove ex-dividend/ex-rights gaps.
- *Useful for*: technical analysis, chart patterns, current price reference, setting alerts, visual continuity.
- *Caveat*: qfq history *changes* whenever a new dividend/split occurs, because the anchor is the present. So cached qfq data becomes stale.

*** 后复权 (hfq) — “backward/post adjustment”
- *Anchor*: the *earliest* price is unchanged.
- *Later prices* are adjusted upward to reflect cumulative dividends/splits.
- *Useful for*: backtesting, performance measurement, total return calculation, long-term charts, comparing cumulative returns across stocks.
- *Caveat*: hfq prices are not real traded prices and are often much higher than raw.

*** Simple example (one cash dividend, D=5)
Raw: Day0 = 100, Day1 ex = 95, Day2 = 100.

| Day | Raw | qfq | hfq |
|-----|-----|-----|-----|
| 0   | 100 | 95  | 100 |
| 1   | 95  | 95  | 100 |
| 2   | 100 | 100 | 105.26 |

- qfq removes the drop on Day1; current price stays raw.
- hfq keeps Day0=100 and scales later prices by the cumulative adjustment factor (assuming dividend reinvestment). The total return from Day0 to Day2 is 5.26% in both qfq and hfq.

def trade(as_of):
    "Handles one trading session."

    #1. do market research, determine the watchlist of stocks we should consider.
    watches = research()

** endpoints

Two Tencent endpoints, both verified working from this machine on 2026-09-29, both reached with the standard library (=urllib=, no pandas):

The daily-bar endpoint allows requests for up to 640 entries. Refreshes are paced, quote requests are batched, and retries are bounded so transient connection failures do not immediately abort a refresh.

** requests

Both endpoints use the same HTTP request approach. Pacing separates symbol requests, and exponential retry delays give transient connection failures a chance to recover without leaving a partial snapshot unnoticed.

=RemoteDisconnected= is a subclass of =OSError=, so the retry catches the same failures that make the eastmoney route hopeless. The difference is that this endpoint answers on the second try.

The endpoints add a prefix for the exchange: 6xxxxx and 9xxxxx are Shanghai, 4xxxxx and 8xxxxx Beijing, everything else Shenzhen.

** refresh

The writers own the complete endpoint-to-file operation. =get= fetches the payload; each writer decodes its response and writes the snapshot in one step. The market module only reads these files, so importing =source= or =market= stays offline.

** daily bar snapshots

The endpoint row is =[date, open, close, high, low, volume]=. The CSV row is =[date, open, high, low, close, volume]=; the writer rearranges the prices and converts volume from a decimal string such as =222233.000= to an integer. No intermediate market objects are constructed.

** instrument names

=names.csv= is one shared file, so a subset refresh must preserve names for symbols not included in the request. The quote endpoint batches symbols and returns fixed-width, semicolon-separated records; the refresh parses these records, merges fetched names over the stored mapping, and writes the merged table.

Names are padded to fit the quote protocol's fixed-width fields, so whitespace is removed inside each name as it is parsed. Fetched names win; names outside a subset refresh remain in the merged table.

Refresh rewrites full raw and qfq histories because an adjustment can revise old rows. Name updates merge with the existing table so refreshing a subset does not erase names for the rest of the watchlist.

** network boundary and CLI

symbol -> market_code -> HTTP request -> response text -> CSV rows/names -> snapshot.

This is the only module in the project that opens a socket or writes the snapshot. Its CLI calls =refresh= explicitly; market reads the resulting files offline.

Running =python source.py= executes the live fetch checks, which write only to temporary test directories. Persisting refreshed market data requires the explicit =refresh= command; a bare run never rewrites the local snapshot.

** read snapshot into market types

=Money= and =Quantity= live here rather than in =book=, because a price is market data before it is account state. We simply use floats for money.

=volume= is in 手 -- lots of 100 shares. The date is a trading day because the source is end-of-day. The reader preserves oldest-first order, so the first and last elements have stable meanings without sorting at each call site.

** gather: point-in-time market

=mark= is the single definition of the current price. A missing symbol raises =KeyError=: a holding absent from the market is an upstream problem, not a zero-valued position.

The default window is a modelling choice, not a property of the stored snapshot. Keep the full history on disk; =gather= selects the recent window passed downstream. At 60 trading days, the current momentum window is roughly a quarter; =window=None= exposes all stored history.

=gather= reads only local files. =as_of= filters out later rows and omits symbols with no history by that date. =window= limits the rows passed downstream, while raw and adjusted bars are intersected by date so marks and returns refer to matching days.

The booking system consists of three parts: the static account (holding at any specific moment), the accumulating ledger (the total trading history), and how to replay the ledger to get the account.

=replay(ledger, as_of)= answers "what did the account look like then" from the same tuple at any moment, which is what a backtest needs and what keeps the state from drifting away from what was actually executed.

We currently only have one ledger, which opens with one 50K cash flow and no positions. Future fills will go into the ledger, then replayed into the account.

One account contains multiple positions, and each position is a *bag of lots* rather than a quantity and an average price.

** the account

An Account is a point-in-time holding: cash, plus the open positions, plus the derived numbers the decision layer reads -- equity, marked value, weights.

A Position records all the trading actions (in Lots) executed up to that moment, plus the reference price it is marked at.

Lot, Position, and Account are all frozen dataclasses. Immutability makes them better suited for functional programming, and makes the replays a safer operation.

The purchase date is per lot rather than per position, which is what makes T+1 exact: a position accumulated across two days is only partly sellable on the second.

=price= is the mark the position was valued at, supplied by the caller because the book does not know about markets. Storing it rather than looking it up makes =value= a total property, so one position cannot be priced two different ways inside one session.

=available= is *strictly* before =as_of=, so a lot bought today is not sellable today; and the date is a parameter rather than a clock reading, so the same position answers for any past or future moment. =cost= is a weighted average over the lots and is derived rather than stored -- no stored average can disagree with the lots it came from.

There is no duplicate-symbol check since Positions come from =replay='s dict of symbol to lots, and a dict cannot contain a duplicate key.

=weight= and =cash_weight= are 0 for an emptied account rather than a division error. Equity *is* cash plus marked value, so zero equity means every weight is zero; dividing anyway would raise where the state itself is perfectly describable.

** the ledger

The ledger is stored as a flow of historical cash and Fill operations, and every snapshot is derived from it.
=Ledger= is a plain tuple, not a class: order is the only structure it has, and =replay= is the only operation.

A deposit and a withdrawal are the same event with opposite signs, so one entry type covers both -- and, later, dividends and fees. Each flow carries its own date, independent of the dates of other flows.

=Side= belongs here rather than beside =Lot=, because nothing about a lot needs a direction: a lot is always something you bought. A =Fill= is the first type that needs to know which way it went, so =Side= sits directly above it in the dependence tree.

=sign= writes the cash arithmetic once, so =cash_delta= needs no buy/sell branch below: a sale's proceeds are a purchase's cost with the direction flipped.

A fill carries the *executed* price, so it also records the slippage against the order's reference price (=fill.price - order.price=). A partial fill is just a fill: the ledger records what happened, not what was asked for.

The paper-trading ledger is the module's data rather than logic. =LEDGER= currently opens with one 50K cash flow and grows as later fills are appended.

** replay ledger into account

Replay is two independent folds, which is the nicest part of the design.
1. =cash_delta(entry)= gives each entry's signed cash effect, and cash is just their sum.
2. =lot_book(fills)= is the FIFO fold of the fills alone.
3. =replay= is deliberately not given the market, only the two lookups it needs.

Entries dated after =as_of= are ignored, so one ledger answers for any moment -- that is the backtest door.

Cash never needs to know which lot a sale consumed, so this is the sum of the flows rather than a side effect of matching. The =match= makes that literal: the two cases are exhaustive over =Entry=, so there is no branch for "some other kind of entry".

: TODO inspect return type: why converted to tuple when we need list in lot_book? there seems to be some unnecessary roundtrip here. Perhaps we need some methods from lists?

A lot bought on =as_of= is not yet available under T+1, so it is skipped. If the older lots cannot cover the sale, the ledger is wrong and we say so loudly rather than inventing shares.

lot_book processes each fill in order and maintains a per-symbol “open lot” book:
- A *BUY* appends a new open =Lot(date, quantity, price)= for that symbol.
- A *SELL* does not append a lot. Instead it calls =sell_lots(...)= to consume existing lots FIFO for that symbol, then *replaces* the symbol’s list with the remaining lots.
- At the end it converts each symbol’s remaining list of lots to a tuple, and removes symbols with no remaining lots.

The =append= call is applied to the list stored in =book= for =fill.symbol= — not to =book= itself.

Cash is one running balance: the sum of the =CashFlow= entries and the fills.

Two things about the shape are deliberate. The prices and names are keyword-only, so a caller cannot swap them by position. And the filter is =entry.date<=as_of=, not =<=, because an entry dated exactly =as_of= has happened by the end of that day.

the replay keeps two tracks, a cash change, and a lot book; buying inserts lots, selling consumes lots FIFO.

This module is heavy in data type definitions; most of the processing logic belongs to replay.

* beliefs
:PROPERTIES:
:header-args:python: :session beliefs :results none :kernel notes :exports both
:END:

Inference turns the point-in-time market into one belief per watched instrument. Beliefs depend on market data, not on the account; the decision step combines them with holdings and explicit rules.

The first model is momentum over the observed market window: adjusted close at the end divided by adjusted close at the start, minus one. This treats recent performance as evidence about expected return and avoids dividend-related price drops. Each belief also carries the latest raw close as its mark, so orders for unheld instruments have a reference price. It does not model uncertainty or use the current account.

Flow: =Market.adjusted_closes= → window return; =Market.closes= → raw mark → =Belief= per symbol.

The window comes from =gather=. Changing the horizon, combining other signals, or estimating uncertainty are later model choices.

* orders

The decision step combines the account, beliefs, explicit =Rules=, and =as_of= date to produce manual order intentions. Rules are passed in so the human can track and adjust policy. Beliefs carry the current raw mark, including for instruments not already held; positions also retain their account mark. Rebalancing does not execute orders.

The initial rules are a 10% cash reserve, a 15% maximum position weight, 100-share buy and partial-sell lots, and T+1 selling. A positive expected return makes a symbol eligible to buy; a nonpositive or missing belief signals an exit. This is a simple placeholder policy.

The rebalancing flow is:
1. Sell eligible shares in positions with a nonpositive or missing belief, and trim positions over their cap, subject to T+1.
2. Rank positive-belief symbols by expected return.
3. Allocate only current cash above the reserve, respecting the per-position cap and 100-share buy lots. Expected sell proceeds are not included in the buy budget.
4. Return sells before buys.

A full exit may sell the entire available position, including a remainder below 100 shares. A partial sell uses whole lots. An order records symbol, side, quantity, reference price, and reason. A fill is recorded separately after the human places the order; comparing fill and reference prices can later measure slippage.

Whether to report an over-cap remainder that T+1 prevents selling is left for evaluation. Handling failed sells and reallocating their expected proceeds is deferred; buys are budgeted from current cash only.
* evaluations

Evaluation describes the current account and proposed batch so the human can review risk before acting. Begin with equity, cash weight, largest position weight, and rule violations. Historical performance, drawdown, attribution, and benchmark comparison need an explicit time-series design and remain open decisions.

render: the ticket

The report presents the risk picture first, then a readable ticket with symbol names, side, quantity, reference price, and reason. It ends with the cash released or used by the batch. Formatting and evaluation stay separate from placing orders.
